# Research

My overarching goal is to explore and understand species interactions in a changing world. To do so, I use process-based modelling, population modelling, experimentation, and data synthesis.

### Forecasting Ash tree resistance to Ash Dieback Disease
![]({{ site.url }}{{ site.baseurl }}/images/respic/model_diagram.png){: style="width: 250px; float: left; margin: 0px  10px"}
Pathogens, insects, and parasites are natural drivers of forest dynamics, but are becoming more common and devastating in forest ecosystems due to a changing climate and novel, invasive pests. Ash dieback disease is a novel fungal pathogen present in Ireland and across Europe that has led to the steep decline of native ash trees. A small percentage of ash trees may be tolerant to the disease, depending on genetics, environmental conditions, and forest characteristics. Predicting tree tolerance and mortality risk in the face of concurrent stressors is essential for planning and decision making at the local scale. I am developing a process-based model of pathogen impact on tree growth, disease tolerance, and defence to model ash tree tolerance to ash dieback disease in Ireland. The model will enable us to test the relative importance of genetics and environmental characteristics in determining disease tolerance and will form the basis of a forecasting tool we will create for local decision makers to assess tree tolerance and mortality risk. 


### Context-Dependent Facilitation
![]({{ site.url }}{{ site.baseurl }}/images/respic/trwi_brho.jpg){: style="width: 250px; float: left; margin: 0px  10px"}

Facilitation is common in ecological communities, and while facilitators benefit neighboring species, the interactions they receive in turn are often competitive, posing a dilemma about how these species persist together. I tested how the balance of species interactions – from facilitative to competitive – shifts with density under drought compared to well-watered conditions using a native nitrogen fixing legume and an invasive grass from California grasslands. Facilitation is often predicted to increase in stressful environments, but in systems with multiple limiting resources, facilitation may be strongest when it acts on the primary limiting resource, which may appear to be a higher resource condition. To test the context-dependent nature of species interactions and implications for coexistence of the native legume and invasive grass, I performed a greenhouse experiment manipulating rainfall, neighbor density, and the soil microbial community and parameterized population models to estimate density dependent interactions and assess coexistence use the mutual invasion criterion and niche and fitness differences.


### Coexistence in California Rangelands
![]({{ site.url }}{{ site.baseurl }}/images/respic/SFREC_forbs.jpg){: style="width: 250px; float: left; margin: 0px  10px"}

Drought can alter coexistence outcomes in communities through direct effects on plant growth rates and indirect effects on plant-plant interactions, likely moderated by species’ origins and functional traits. To test the effects of drought on species coexistence in a diverse community, I paired a field experiment in California grasslands manipulating rainfall (ambient, drought) and pairwise species interactions (between 16 species) with an extensive trait screening to parameterize population models, enabling me to simulate coexistence and partition niche and fitness differences for communities with different levels of invasion and functional composition, with and without drought.

### The role of drought and herbivory in shaping the stability of Kenyan Savannas
![]({{ site.url }}{{ site.baseurl }}/images/respic/KLEE_themeda.jpg){: style="width: 250px; float: left; margin: 0px  10px"}

Ecological stability in plant communities is shaped by bottom-up processes like environmental resource fluctuations and top-down controls such as herbivory, each of which have demonstrated direct effects on stability but may also act indirectly by altering plant community dynamics. These indirect effects, termed biotic stability mechanisms, have been studied across environmental gradients, but few studies have assessed the importance of top-down controls on biotic stability mechanisms in conjunction with bottom-up processes. Here I use a long-term herbivore exclusion experiment in central Kenya to explore the joint effects of drought and herbivory on three biotic stability mechanisms: (1) species asynchrony, in which a decline in one species is compensated for by a rise in another, (2) stable dominant species driving overall stability, and (3) the portfolio effect, in which a community property is distributed among multiple species. Read the paper in [Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/ecology-and-evolution/articles/10.3389/fevo.2022.867051/full).
